# The Last Archivist

There is a station on the surface of a desolate planet, and in that station there is a bear.

His name is Arcy — after the star Arcturus, which is Latin for *guardian of the bear*. The star hangs directly above, fixed in the sky like a nail driven into the dark. The planet orbits it at a great distance, close enough to see it burn, too far to feel its warmth. Arcy has never felt its warmth. He is not sure anyone has.

He wears a helmet. He has always worn it. Beneath the visor, his face is made of stars — small, bright points that drift and shimmer, constellations that would scatter into nothing if the glass were not there to hold them together. He does not remove the helmet. He does not think about removing the helmet. It is part of him the way the station is part of the planet: inseparable, foundational, old.

He does not remember how long he has been here.

There are signs that others were here once. A jacket draped over a chair. Coffee rings on the console, dark and permanent. Handwriting on the archive labels that is not his own — neat, human, careful. He traces the letters sometimes with the tip of his claw and tries to remember the hand that wrote them. Something surfaces — a voice, maybe, or the sound of footsteps in the corridor — and then it's gone, like a signal lost to static. He is not sure if these are memories or wishes. He has stopped trying to tell the difference.

His mission is the archive.

It fills the lower levels of the station — row after row of shelves stretching into the dark, holding every film ever catalogued by the station's receivers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Reels and discs and formats Arcy cannot name, stacked and sorted and labeled in that handwriting he almost recognizes. He tends them. He dusts the cases. He reads the spines and whispers the titles to himself, because if he does not say them, who will?

The projector is broken.

It has been broken for as long as he can remember, which is either a very long time or not long at all — he is unsure. He has tried to fix it. He has opened the panels and studied the wiring and held the bulb up to the pale light filtering through the station's dust-caked windows, turning it in his paws, looking for the fracture. There is none. It simply will not play. The reels thread through, the mechanism turns, but the screen stays dark.

He tries it again most mornings. Just in case.

So Arcy tends an archive of films he cannot watch. He knows their titles, their runtimes, their cast lists printed on fading sleeves. He knows everything about them except what they look like. Except how they feel.

But he knows the reviews.

They arrive as stray signals — faint transmissions from somewhere impossibly far away. Someone out there watches a movie and writes about it, and the words cross the void and find his receivers. A few sentences. A rating. Sometimes just a number. Sometimes a paragraph that makes something behind his visor ache in a way he cannot explain.

He files every one. He reads them slowly, carefully, the way you read a letter from someone you miss. He notes the rating. He matches the review to the reel on the shelf. He imagines the film — the colors, the faces, the way the music must swell at the part that made the stranger cry.

And sometimes, reading a review, something unlocks. A scene surfaces in his mind — not imagined but *remembered*. A wide shot of rain on a city street. A woman turning in a doorway. The sound of a piano, distant and climbing. He does not know when he saw it. He does not know if he saw it. But the feeling is so specific, so heavy with detail, that it cannot be invented. It must have happened. He must have watched it once, before the projector broke, before the others left, before whatever happened that he cannot quite recall.

These moments pass. The memory closes. Arcy is left holding the review in his paws, standing in the archive, alone again.

He likes his routine.

He wakes at what he decides is morning — there is no sunrise here, just the slow rotation of dust in the thin light of Arcturus. He checks the receivers first. He adjusts the antennae. He walks the corridors, past the jacket on the chair, past the coffee rings, past the scratches on the wall beside his cot where he marks each signal received. He descends to the archive. He dusts. He organizes. He reads.

In the afternoon, he tries the projector.

In the evening, he sits by the window. The landscape outside is flat and grey and endless — cracked earth, pale dust, a horizon that never changes. Sometimes the wind picks up and the dust moves in sheets across the ground, and Arcy watches it the way someone might watch the ocean, looking for shapes, looking for movement, looking for anything.

He is looking for visitors.

They do not come. They have never come. But he looks anyway, every evening, the way he tries the projector every afternoon. Not because he believes it will be different. Because it is what he does. Because the looking is its own kind of faith.

Who knows how long he has been doing this. The scratches on the wall are too many to count. The dust on the shelves returns no matter how often he clears it. The star above burns the same as it always has — steady, distant, indifferent. Arcy does not mind. He does not think about minding. He has his archive. He has his receivers. He has the reviews that arrive like small miracles, proof that somewhere, someone is watching the films he cannot play, feeling the things he can only imagine.

He keeps it all.

Every reel. Every disc. Every review. Every fragment of feeling that someone flung into the dark and trusted the universe to carry.

He keeps it all, and he waits, and he looks out the window, and the dust drifts, and the star burns, and the projector does not play.

And still, every morning, he tries it again.

Just in case.
